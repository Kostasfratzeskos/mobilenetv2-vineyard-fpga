\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Grapevine is a profitable crop that numerous diseases can affect, which makes
their effective detection essential for crop
monitoring~\cite{morellos2024iot}. Deep learning provides an alternative to
traditional detection methods: convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can
identify plant diseases from
images~\cite{mohanty2016plantdisease,liu2020grape}, and lightweight networks
of this kind have been deployed on edge devices and validated in the
field~\cite{morellos2024iot}. The convolutions at the core of these networks,
however, are computationally intensive and often require a powerful platform
such as a graphics processing unit (GPU), which makes CNNs difficult to deploy
on portable devices~\cite{bai2018dwsep}.

This thesis designs, verifies and integrates a hardware accelerator for
MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} inference on a Zynq UltraScale+
multiprocessor system-on-chip (MPSoC), targeting the XCZU7EV device of the
ZCU104 evaluation board. The accelerator classifies a
$\thImgSize \times \thImgSize$ RGB image of a grapevine leaf into one of
\thClasses{} classes: black rot, esca, leaf blight and healthy. It is written
by hand in Verilog-2001 at the register-transfer level (RTL) and performs
integer-only inference~\cite{jacob2018quantization} on int8 weights and
activations, with per-channel weight scales and per-tensor activation scales.
A program-driven controller sequences a small set of reusable, parameterized
compute engines; the weights are streamed from off-chip DDR memory layer by
layer, whereas the activations remain on chip for the entire inference.

The work is empirical throughout. An integer-only software model of the
network emits the exact integer output of every layer, referred to as the
\emph{golden vectors}, and every RTL block, as well as the assembled network,
is checked against these vectors bit for bit. A hardware result therefore
either matches the software model exactly or constitutes a defect, and a
failure can be localized to a single layer; Chapter~\ref{chap:verification}
develops this method. Design decisions are defended in the same manner: with
measured data or, where the correctness of the design depends on them, with
provable bounds rather than observed values.

The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:motivation} motivates the work, from the impact of grapevine
diseases to the case for dedicated inference hardware.
Section~\ref{sec:objectives} states the objectives of the thesis as three tiers
of success criteria and sets out its scope, Section~\ref{sec:contributions}
summarizes its contributions, and Section~\ref{sec:outline} outlines the
remaining chapters.


\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The motivation for this thesis rests on three observations. First, grapevine
diseases affect an economically significant crop whose protection relies
heavily on fungicides~\cite{oiv2026state,eurostat2007ppp}. Second,
image-based classifiers have made automated disease detection feasible,
including on edge devices in the
field~\cite{mohanty2016plantdisease,morellos2024iot}. Third, the computational
cost of these classifiers motivates their execution on dedicated
hardware~\cite{bai2018dwsep,guo2019survey}.
Sections~\ref{sec:motivation-diseases},~\ref{sec:motivation-detection}
and~\ref{sec:motivation-hardware} develop these observations in turn.


\subsection{Grapevine Diseases and Their Impact}
\label{sec:motivation-diseases}

Viticulture is a sector of considerable economic scale: according to the
report of the International Organisation of Vine and Wine (OIV) on the year
2025, the world vineyard area amounted to 7.0 million hectares, approximately
45\% of which lay in the European Union~\cite{oiv2026state}. In the same year,
world wine production was estimated at 227 million hectolitres, and the value
of international wine exports at EUR~33.8 billion~\cite{oiv2026state}.

The crop is, moreover, exposed to a large number of threats: a reference
compendium describes nearly seventy diseases, disorders and pests of
grape~\cite{wilcox2015compendium}. Several of them affect the leaves:
Liu et al.~\cite{liu2020grape} identify six common grape leaf diseases and
pests, among them black rot and leaf blight, as causes of severe economic
losses to the grape industry, while Xie et al.~\cite{xie2020deep} report that
black rot, black measles (also named esca), leaf blight and mites seriously
affect grape yield. The first three of these are the disease classes of this
thesis. Beyond the foliage, fungal trunk diseases rank among the most
destructive diseases of grapevine in all grape-growing regions, and no single
effective control measure against them has been
developed~\cite{gramaje2018managing}.

In practice, disease control in viticulture relies heavily on chemical
protection: in the estimates that Eurostat compiled for 1992--2003, vines
received between 64\% and 75\% of the fungicides used each year in the
agriculture of the European Union, measured by mass of active substance,
while occupying between 3\% and 5\% of the area under the crops that the same
data cover~\cite{eurostat2007ppp}.
% Both ranges are computed from the book's own tables, year by year: 2.1.2
% (all fungicides), 5.7.1 (fungicides on grapes and vines) and 2.5.1 (crop
% areas). Its prose states neither figure.
Reducing pesticide use on grapevine is the subject of a critical review of
plant-protection tools and of the prospects for implementing integrated pest
management (IPM) in viticulture~\cite{pertot2017critical}. In parallel, timely
diagnosis and accurate identification are regarded as decisive for
controlling the spread of grape leaf diseases~\cite{liu2020grape}. The images
of these diseases on which this thesis works are described in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset}.


\subsection{Image-Based Disease Detection}
\label{sec:motivation-detection}

Deep learning has been applied to image-based plant disease detection and
diagnosis~\cite{mohanty2016plantdisease,ferentinos2018deep}, and its use
across agriculture has been surveyed by Kamilaris and
Prenafeta-Bold\'u~\cite{kamilaris2018deep}. Hughes and Salath\'e released an
open-access repository of plant-health images, PlantVillage, specifically to
enable the development of mobile disease
diagnostics~\cite{hughes2015plantvillage}; the images used in this thesis are
drawn from it (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}). Studies of grape leaf diseases
observe that the identification of early plant diseases with CNNs has become
a research focus~\cite{liu2020grape,xie2020deep}.

Grape-specific models have also been
developed~\cite{liu2020grape,xie2020deep}. Liu et al.\ combined 4,023 images
collected in the field with 3,646 images from public datasets, generated a
dataset of 107,366 grape leaf images through image enhancement techniques, and
trained from scratch a CNN, DICNN, that combines Inception structures with
dense connectivity~\cite{liu2020grape}. Xie et al.\ expanded grape leaf
disease images through digital image processing to construct a grape leaf
disease dataset (GLDD), and proposed a real-time detector for grape leaf
diseases based on improved deep CNNs~\cite{xie2020deep}.

For embedded platforms, state-of-the-art networks such as
MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} replace standard convolution with
depthwise separable convolution, which significantly reduces the number of
operations and parameters with only a limited loss of
accuracy~\cite{bai2018dwsep}. Morellos et al.\ applied two such lightweight
models to grapevine within an Internet of Things (IoT) approach: they trained
MobileNetV2 and EfficientNet~B0 by transfer learning on a commercially
available online dataset, deployed them on a Raspberry Pi~4 edge device
equipped with an RGB camera, and validated them on site in an organic
winery~\cite{morellos2024iot}. During validation, MobileNetV2 achieved an
accuracy of 94\%, against 92\% for EfficientNet~B0, and an inference time of
\SI{330}{\milli\second}, against \SI{390}{\milli\second}; it was therefore
preferred for online deployment~\cite{morellos2024iot}. Similarly, Karim et
al.\ deployed a lightweight CNN derived from MobileNetV3-Large for grape leaf
disease classification on an edge device, a Jetson Nano, and noted that
inference on the device itself gives low latency, conserves bandwidth and
requires neither an internet connection nor cloud
processing~\cite{karim2024grapeedge}. The IoT service of Morellos et al.\
defines the application context of the present thesis, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:related} reviews it, together with the wider literature on
plant disease detection, in greater depth.


\subsection{The Case for Dedicated Hardware}
\label{sec:motivation-hardware}

These results establish that a lightweight CNN can classify grapevine leaves
on an edge device~\cite{morellos2024iot,karim2024grapeedge}. The cost of
inference is nevertheless high: the computation and storage complexity of
neural network inference complicates its application, and it is difficult for
CPU platforms to provide sufficient computational
capacity~\cite{guo2019survey}. In practice, GPUs are the first choice for
neural network processing, owing to their computational capacity and
easy-to-use development frameworks~\cite{guo2019survey}.

Field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) offer an alternative: with hardware
designed specifically for the task, an FPGA-based accelerator is a possible
means of surpassing the GPU in both speed and energy efficiency, and various
such accelerators have been proposed, combining software and hardware
optimization techniques~\cite{guo2019survey}. The efficient processing of deep
neural networks is surveyed more broadly by Sze et
al.~\cite{sze2017efficient}. Representative designs have optimized FPGA
accelerators for deep CNNs~\cite{zhang2015optimizing} and targeted embedded
FPGA platforms~\cite{qiu2016goingdeeper}, and a range of toolflows automates
the mapping of CNNs onto FPGAs~\cite{venieris2018toolflows}.

MobileNetV2 lends itself well to this approach: Bai et al.\ observe that
networks built on depthwise separable convolution are highly structured and
therefore very suitable for FPGA implementation, and their scalable
accelerator, instantiated for MobileNetV2 on an Arria~10 SoC FPGA, classified
an ImageNet image in \SI{3.75}{\milli\second} (approximately 266.6 frames per
second), a 20-fold speedup over a CPU~\cite{bai2018dwsep}. Wu et al.\
separated the two forms of convolution in hardware, with one dedicated engine
for pointwise and another for depthwise convolution, and executed MobileNetV2
with 8-bit weights and activations on two Zynq UltraScale+ MPSoC devices, the
ZU2 and the ZU9~\cite{wu2019high}. Low-precision execution of this kind relies
on quantization, which enables efficient inference using integer arithmetic
only~\cite{jacob2018quantization,krishnamoorthi2018whitepaper}. FPGAs have
also been applied to plant disease identification itself: Perumal et al.\
used the parallel processing capabilities of a PYNQ FPGA platform to
accelerate CNN inference, with the aim of improving the efficiency of plant
disease detection in agricultural settings~\cite{perumal2024cnn}. Finally,
for the Zynq UltraScale+ family, the vendor offers a ready-made option, the
DPUCZDX8G, an IP core for deep neural network applications that is integrated
into a design through either the Vivado or the Vitis
flow~\cite{xilinx_pg338}.

Apart from the PYNQ study, the works closest to this thesis approach the
problem from one of two directions: grapevine disease classifiers executed on
edge
devices~\cite{morellos2024iot,karim2024grapeedge}, and FPGA accelerators for
MobileNetV2 demonstrated on general-purpose image classification, such as
ImageNet~\cite{bai2018dwsep,wu2019high}. This thesis combines the two: it
implements a MobileNetV2 grapevine disease classifier as a dedicated
accelerator on a Zynq UltraScale+ MPSoC. Rather than configuring a vendor IP
core~\cite{xilinx_pg338} or generating the hardware with a
toolflow~\cite{venieris2018toolflows}, it implements the accelerator by hand
in Verilog-2001, because the layer-level control and understanding that
hand-written RTL gives are the purpose of the work. Whether such an
accelerator is preferable to the edge devices on which grapevine classifiers
are already deployed~\cite{morellos2024iot,karim2024grapeedge} is, however, a
question for measurement rather than for citation.
Section~\ref{sec:objectives} therefore includes that comparison among the
objectives of the thesis, alongside the tiers that define its success.


\section{Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objective of this thesis is an accelerator that reproduces the
integer-only software model of MobileNetV2 bit for bit and classifies
grapevine leaves on the ZCU104 board. A complete network and a live camera,
both to be developed within the duration of a diploma thesis, form a
high-risk target if treated as all or nothing. The objective was therefore
divided at the outset into three cumulative tiers of success criteria, so
that a defensible result would exist even if the work on the board ran late
(\DD{004}). Decisions are cited throughout by their identifiers in the design
decision log, which Appendix~\hyperref[appendix:decisions]{B} summarizes. Each
tier is defined below, together with the measure that decides it and its
status at the time of writing.

\begin{description}
  \item[Foundation.] The complete MobileNetV2 network runs bit-exact against
    its software model in simulation. The measure is the element-by-element
    comparison of every intermediate tensor of a whole-network simulation with
    the golden vectors, \thElements{} elements over the \thInstructions{}
    instructions of the program, in which a single differing element is a
    failure. \emph{Status:} reached. With the deployed model, the
    whole-network comparison is bit-exact on \thImagesVerified{} images, one
    per class, and all \thTestbenches{} testbenches of the verification suite
    pass (Section~\ref{sec:bit-exactness-result}).
  \item[Core.] An image loaded from the SD card is classified by the
    accelerator on the board itself; this tier is the minimum that the thesis
    must meet. The measure is a known answer, staged on the card together
    with the image: the \thClasses{} integer logits and the class that the
    simulation produces for that image, which the board must reproduce bit for
    bit. \emph{Status:} reached on 7~October 2026, when the bare-metal
    application on the ZCU104, booted over JTAG with the image read from the
    SD card, reproduced the known answer of one image bit for bit
    (Section~\ref{sec:bringup}). On 22~October 2026, under Linux, the same
    driver reproduced the known answers of all \thClasses{} classes
    (Section~\ref{sec:bit-exactness-result}).
  \item[Stretch.] A live camera feeds the accelerator, which classifies its
    frames on the board in real time. The measures are the known answers,
    which establish that the board computes exactly what the simulation
    computes; the frame rate and the per-frame time of the live loop; and a
    check that frames saved on the board, when re-derived on the host, yield
    the same resized window, input tensor and logits. \emph{Status:} reached
    on 22~October 2026, when the frames of a USB webcam connected to the
    board were classified live under Linux
    (Section~\ref{sec:camera-result}). The frame rate of the live loop was
    not recorded during that run, however, so the real-time qualifier is not
    yet supported by a measured figure, and neither the check of saved frames
    nor the display output has yet been verified.
\end{description}

All the results on the board quoted above were obtained with the weights of
an earlier training run of the same network. The model was subsequently
retrained on a split of the dataset by leaf (\DD{034}) and redeployed to the
accelerator without any change to the RTL, to the compiled program or to the
cycle count of an inference (\DD{036}). The redeployed model is bit-exact in
simulation, and its known answers are staged for the board; at the time of
writing, however, it has not yet been run on the board.

Two constraints bound the work. The first concerns the method: the
accelerator, including its weight DMA engine and its control registers, is
written by hand in Verilog-2001 (\DD{002}). It is neither generated by
high-level synthesis nor assembled from a vendor accelerator core, because
the layer-level control and understanding that hand-written RTL gives are
what the work is about; Section~\ref{sec:hls} discusses the alternative, and
Section~\ref{sec:positioning} positions the thesis against it. The second
constraint concerns the data, and it is stated here as a limit of every
result that follows. The dataset is final (\DD{036}): the grape leaf images
of PlantVillage~\cite{hughes2015plantvillage}, split into training,
validation and test sets by leaf rather than by image. The dataset holds
several photographs of each leaf, as Section~\ref{sec:leakage} shows, and a
split by image therefore separates photographs of the same leaf between
training and evaluation, as the earlier splits of this work did (\DD{034}).
The test split is scored once, after the checkpoint and its int8 scales have
been fixed, and no photographs taken in a vineyard are used. Since the
PlantVillage images were collected under controlled
conditions~\cite{mohanty2016plantdisease}, every accuracy figure in this
thesis describes images of that kind, and none yet describes photographs
from a vineyard; such photographs are left to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:future}).

Beyond the tiers, the evaluation is to compare the accelerator with two edge
platforms of the kind on which grapevine classifiers have been deployed, a
Raspberry Pi~4~\cite{morellos2024iot} and a Jetson
Nano~\cite{karim2024grapeedge} (\DD{036}). The comparison is designed so that
only the platform differs: the same trained model, the same
\thAccTestImages{} test images, the same preprocessing and the same
measurement procedure, including a single power meter for all three boards.
It comprises two comparisons, which are never combined into one figure. The
first runs the same integer arithmetic on the Raspberry Pi~4, through the
bit-exact C model of Section~\ref{sec:c-model}, and therefore isolates the
effect of the architecture. The second runs the software stack of each
platform at its best available precision, and therefore reflects what a
deployment on that platform would obtain. At the time of writing, none of
these measurements has been made; Section~\ref{sec:comparison} presents the
comparison.


\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The contributions of this thesis are listed below, each with the chapter or
section that presents it. Results obtained on the board are stated together
with the model that produced them, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An integer-only MobileNetV2 accelerator for the Zynq UltraScale+
    MPSoC, written by hand in Verilog-2001. A program of \thInstructions{}
    instructions drives five parameterized compute engines, which share a
    single requantization stage and a single on-chip activation pool, while
    the weights are streamed from DDR memory; the finished accelerator uses
    \thDspAccelAxi{} DSP slices, \thDspPctAccelAxi\% of those of the device
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture}).
  \item A verification method in which the hardware must reproduce the golden
    vectors of the integer-only software model bit for bit, at the level of
    each engine, of each layer and of the whole network. With the deployed
    model, the whole-network comparison covers \thElements{} elements and is
    bit-exact on \thImagesVerified{} images, one per class
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:verification}, Section~\ref{sec:bit-exactness-result}).
  \item Datapath widths that rest on provable bounds rather than on
    observations. In particular, the accumulator width is sized from a
    worst-case bound that holds for any input; the exporter recomputes this
    bound for every exported model and checks it against the width read from
    the RTL, and when the model was retrained on the split by leaf, the check
    reported, without any manual step, that the bound had moved to
    \thAccBoundBits{} bits (Section~\ref{sec:acc-bound},
    Section~\ref{sec:widths}).
  \item A software reference model, and an accuracy evaluation that keeps the
    photographs of each leaf on one side of the split. An analysis of the
    earlier splits found photographs of the same leaf on both sides of them;
    on the split by leaf, scored once, the floating-point model classifies
    \thAccFloat\% and the integer pipeline that the accelerator reproduces
    \thAccInt\% of the \thAccTestImages{} test images correctly
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:software}, Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}).
  \item A timing-closure campaign at a fixed clock constraint, in which each
    optimization was implemented and measured separately. Over the steps that
    can be attributed to individual changes, the maximum frequency of the
    out-of-context implementation, derived from its worst negative slack, rose
    from \MHz{\thFmaxBase} to \MHz{\thFmaxPhaseThree}
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:timing}).
  \item The integration of the accelerator into the MPSoC: a hand-written
    weight DMA engine and AXI-Lite control registers, a block design for the
    ZCU104 that meets its timing constraint at \MHz{\thClkBoard}, and a
    processing-system application whose image preprocessing is bit-exact
    against the software model. With the weights of the earlier training run,
    the bare-metal application reproduced the known answer of one image bit
    for bit on the board (Chapter~\ref{chap:integration},
    Section~\ref{sec:bringup}).
  \item A live camera path under Linux, whose front end resizes each camera
    frame with the same result, bit for bit, as the preprocessing of the
    software model, as verified on the host. With the same weights, the board
    reproduced the known answers of all \thClasses{} classes, at a measured
    mean of \SI{\thBoardMs}{\milli\second} per image over \thBoardRuns{} runs
    from the start of the image transfer to the result, and then classified
    the frames of a USB webcam live (Section~\ref{sec:camera-path},
    Section~\ref{sec:camera-result}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows.
Chapter~\ref{chap:related} reviews deep learning for plant disease detection,
plant disease classifiers deployed on edge devices and CNN accelerators on
FPGAs, and positions this thesis with respect to them.
Chapter~\ref{chap:background} then provides the background on which the
subsequent chapters depend: convolutional neural networks and the MobileNetV2
architecture, the quantization of neural networks, FPGAs and the Zynq
UltraScale+ MPSoC, and the design principles of CNN accelerators. The
experimental setup, namely the ZCU104 board, the two edge platforms of the
comparison and the measurement equipment, is described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:setup}, together with the development tools.

The work itself is presented in
Chapters~\ref{chap:software} to~\ref{chap:integration}.
Chapter~\ref{chap:software} presents the software reference model: the
dataset and its split by leaf, the training of the model and its
post-training quantization to int8, the export of its parameters and golden
vectors, the bit-exact C reference model, the input preprocessing, and the
workload analysis that identifies the operations to accelerate. On this
basis, Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} presents the architecture of the
accelerator: its compute engines and their degree of parallelism, its
on-chip memory organization, its control unit and instruction set, and the
latency contract between the feeders and the engines. The verification
methodology, which is built on bit-exactness against the golden vectors, is
described in Chapter~\ref{chap:verification}, together with its limits.
Chapter~\ref{chap:timing} presents the timing closure of the design, one
measured optimization at a time, and the critical path that remains.
Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:integration} integrates the accelerator into the
MPSoC, from the weight DMA and the control registers to the
processing-system application, the bring-up on the board and the live
camera path under Linux.

The results are evaluated in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}: resource
utilization and clock frequency, throughput and latency, bit-exactness and
classification accuracy, power consumption, the comparison with the edge
platforms and with related work, and the live camera demonstration.
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} assesses the thesis against the objectives of
Section~\ref{sec:objectives} and identifies future work. Three appendices
complete the thesis: Appendix~\hyperref[appendix:isa]{A} specifies the
instruction set of the accelerator and lists the compiled program,
Appendix~\hyperref[appendix:decisions]{B} summarizes the design decision log,
and Appendix~\hyperref[appendix:reproduction]{C} describes how the design is
built and how each result, including every figure quoted in the text, is
reproduced.
